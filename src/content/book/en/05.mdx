# 5. Monitoring: making impact visible

Monitoring means documenting progress and impact so that an initiative can learn, communicate and make political arguments. It does not need to be complicated – and it doesn't have to be left to experts. Self-initiated research has its own validity: it shows decision-makers where to look and lets them see a street through a different lens.

  Connect data and storytelling.
</PullQuote>

## Four ways to collect data

Prostorož in Ljubljana combines different methods depending on the question – and on the people it wants to reach.

<Accordion title="1. Surveying">Ask people directly how they use a place. When Prostorož surveyed visitors to a health care clinic about how they got there, the answer was clear: <Spoiler lang="en">64% came by car</Spoiler> – a strong starting point for a discussion about access and mobility.</Accordion>
<Accordion title="2. Mapping">Let people mark places on a map: where it is too hot, too dangerous, too loud – or where they like to spend time. Free apps such as Canvis or Mapperoni make <DefinitionTerm term="hot spot mapping" definition="Collecting people's experiences of specific places on a shared map to reveal patterns, e.g. heat islands or dangerous crossings." /> easy. Timing matters: if you are mapping urban heat, launch the survey on days above 30 °C, when people feel the problem most.</Accordion>
<Accordion title="3. Collective mind maps">Collecting ideas together on a large sheet is fun and brings out a wide range of views. Keep in mind that the results are harder to translate into formal data.</Accordion>
<Accordion title="4. Combining physical and digital">Put something into the street and ask for feedback at the same time. In Ljubljana, test benches were set up and passers-by could submit feedback on them – the intervention itself became the survey.</Accordion>

## Observing life on the street

Counting and observing show what actually happens in a street – not just what people say.

<FeatureBox title={"Pedestrian flows"}>
  On a busy street in Ljubljana, volunteers counted pedestrians in 15-minute intervals and noted the directions they walked in. They also mapped where people stayed – and found that people could only stand, because there was nowhere to sit.
</FeatureBox>

<FeatureBox title={"Park Beat"}>
  Which car parks around the city centre are actually used? Observations showed that some were more than 100% occupied, while others had spare capacity. Comparing this with the economic activity of nearby shops helps challenge the assumption that every parking space is essential for business.
</FeatureBox>

<FeatureBox title={"Public Life app"}>
  The free Public Life app by Gehl and Urban95 is based on the <DefinitionTerm term="Public Life Data Protocol" definition="An open standard by the Gehl Institute for collecting comparable data on how people move through and stay in public spaces." />. Plan for 5–6 volunteers per street and an observation window from early morning (5 am) until midnight. You don't need many days, but you will need spreadsheets to analyse the results.
</FeatureBox>

## What can be measured

Simple indicators work well for superblocks, tactical urbanism and street design:

- **Traffic volumes:** How many cars, bicycles and pedestrians use a section?
- **Speed and conflicts:** Where do people drive too fast, and where do dangerous situations arise?
- **Use for spending time:** Do people stop or sit down, do children play, do conversations emerge?
- **Parking:** Which spaces are actually used, and when?
- **Climate and comfort:** Is there shade, urban heat, greenery, drinking water or noise reduction?
- **Support:** How many people sign, attend meetings, share content or offer help?
- **Political progress:** What motions, discussions, commitments or administrative reviews are taking place?

<TableWrap>

| Indicator | Simple method | Good result format |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Through traffic | Count at two fixed time windows | Figure, photo, map |
| Pedestrian flows | Counts in 15-minute intervals, incl. direction | Flow map |
| Quality of stay | On-site observation, mapping where people stay | Short notes, quotes, map |
| Parking | Occupancy counts at different times | Occupancy chart |
| Heat and comfort | Hot spot mapping, shade map, photo points | Heat map, before-and-after photo pair |
| Support | Conversations, signatures, meetings | List with roles and contacts |

</TableWrap>

## From data to change

Data alone rarely convinces anyone. Prostorož's approach combines three steps: **activating, envisioning and patiently waiting.** In one action, kayak stands, a red fabric roof and a few folding chairs turned five parking spaces into a place to stay. Paint can rezone a street for a day. And visualisations that respond directly to what the research revealed make the case far better than a table of numbers.

<FeatureBox title="Four lessons from Ljubljana" tone="warm">
  1. **Connect data and storytelling.** Numbers become powerful when they are part of a story people recognise.
  2. **Combine physical interventions with long-term visions.** A temporary action shows what is possible; the vision explains where it leads.
  3. **Steal from the "bad side".** Use the tools of marketing: strong images, simple messages, good timing.
  4. **Take a lot of photos.** You will need them – for reports, social media, press and political talks.
</FeatureBox>

## A minimal monitoring plan

1. **Document the baseline:** photos, a map, a short problem note and an initial count.
2. **Define a target:** for example, less through traffic, more safe crossings or more supporters.
3. **Choose a method:** survey, mapping, traffic or pedestrian count, observation, street radar or before-and-after photos.
4. **Pick the right moment:** heat on hot days, traffic at rush hour, play after school.
5. **Assign responsibilities:** one person coordinates data, one photos, and one quotes and contacts.
6. **Present results:** One page is often enough – problem, method, key figures, photos and demand.
7. **Learn:** What needs adjusting in your demand, action or alliance-building?

<FeatureBox title={"Start small"}>
  Robust monitoring does not need to be scientifically perfect. To start, a repeatable method is often enough: same place, same time, same observation question.
</FeatureBox>

## Monitoring as mobilisation

Monitoring can itself be an action. A street traffic count gets people talking. Test benches with a feedback form invite passers-by to have their say. A map of hot or dangerous spots can attract new supporters – and every person who joins a count is a potential new active ally.
