# 4. Mobilisation and strategies for change

Mobilisation is more than reach. It is not just about how many people see something, but about why they are willing to act together – and what each of them can actually do. Big changes have rarely come from the top down: women's right to vote, for example, was won by people who organised themselves around a shared cause. The same goes for liveable streets.

<PullQuote cite="Valentina, Changing Cities">
  If we want things to change, we always have to fight for them from the bottom up.
</PullQuote>

## From one initiative to a movement

Changing Cities shows what this can look like. Since 2019, the Berlin organisation has supported residents in founding neighbourhood initiatives for <DefinitionTerm term="Kiezblocks" definition="Berlin's version of superblocks: traffic-calmed neighbourhoods without through traffic." />. Through workshops, events and online formats, it shared its knowledge openly. Around 70 initiatives were founded, many Kiezblocks have been politically approved, and 12 are already being implemented on the streets.<Footnote text="Figures as presented by Changing Cities at the Toolbox for City Changers workshop, 2026." />

Berlin isn't a Kiezblock paradise yet – but every one of these streets shows what organised neighbours can achieve.

## Five tools for organising

### Tool 1: Heart, head, hand

Less a tool than a principle to keep in mind whenever you organise people:

- **Head:** strategy, tactics, arguments. Political work often focuses here.
- **Heart:** what moves people – a grandchild's unsafe route to school, a childhood memory of playing in the street, the fear of getting sick from air pollution.
- **Hand:** always make a concrete offer – a task, an event, something to join.

A movement from below can only grow when all three are in balance.

<FeatureBox title={"Take a minute"}>
  What is your heart? What moves you to keep fighting for more liveable cities? You don't have to share it – just take it with you, and come back to it when you don't know how to carry on.
</FeatureBox>

### Tool 2: Spectrum of allies

Who are you actually trying to reach? Initiatives often focus too much on their opponents. The spectrum of allies sorts the people around you into five groups:

**Active allies → Passive allies → Neutral → Passive opposition → Active opposition**

The aim is not to turn everyone into active allies at once. The key is to **move one group one step closer to you**. Start on the allies' side and leave the opposition aside for now.

<TableWrap>

| Group | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| Active allies | Build strong relationships – above all through personal encounters such as meetings, events and workshops. Give them opportunities to use their hand. |
| Passive allies | Find out what keeps them passive, then offer a small, concrete step – like baking a cake for the next play street. |
| Neutral | They can shift either way. Introduce yourselves: door-knocking, an info stand in your street, flyers. |

</TableWrap>

The opposition is often loud, which makes it seem big. But which group is usually the largest? <Spoiler lang="en">The neutrals – so focus on them and on your passive allies.</Spoiler>

<FeatureBox title={"Try it with your initiative"}>
  Print the spectrum or draw it on a large sheet. Write the actors around your initiative on sticky notes – local politicians, schools, cafés, other initiatives – and place them on the spectrum. Then decide on your first step.
</FeatureBox>

*Example:* In the founding workshop of a German superblock initiative, the group placed a local school – parents and children – between "passive ally" and "neutral". They organised an action day in front of the school. People got to know the initiative, and some joined afterwards.

### Tool 3: One-on-one conversations

<PullQuote>
  One face-to-face conversation is worth more than five WhatsApp messages.
</PullQuote>

Conversations stick in people's minds. They help you find out what moves someone, what makes them angry or hopeful – and to win them over to your <DefinitionTerm term="plan to win" definition="Your initiative's idea of how change will happen, e.g. collecting 1,000 signatures and taking them to local politicians." />.

**When?** At the start of an initiative, to find out which issues really matter in the neighbourhood. Later, to find new members, invite people to an assembly or collect signatures.

**Where?** At the door – or as "street talks" at the bus stop, in front of the supermarket, on the playground.

**How?** Follow these six steps:

<Accordion title="1. Introduction">"Hi, my name is … I live nearby and I'm part of the neighbourhood initiative. Today we're talking to lots of people in our neighbourhood."</Accordion>
<Accordion title="2. Find out what moves the person">Ask how they feel about the situation – "How do you feel about the traffic in our neighbourhood? What would you like to change?" Then go deeper: "What does that mean for you and your family?"</Accordion>
<Accordion title="3. Agitation">Help them see that the status quo is not a given: "Who do you think is responsible? Don't you think it's unfair that cars get so much space and pedestrians so little?"</Accordion>
<Accordion title="4. Your plan to win">Only now is it your turn to talk. Explain what your initiative is planning – a demonstration, a petition, talks with local politicians – and make clear: this will only work if we do it together.</Accordion>
<Accordion title="5. The ask">A direct question: "Will you join? Can I count on you?"</Accordion>
<Accordion title="6. Next step">Always end with a concrete agreement: "See you at the meeting on …?" or "I'll add you to the group now."</Accordion>

<FeatureBox tone="warm">
  **Golden rule: 20% talking, 80% listening.** Go in pairs, prepare a short guideline and practise with a partner beforehand. Always take notes – and collect contact details.
</FeatureBox>

### Tool 4: Contact management

It sounds basic, but it is often underestimated. Collect more than an email address: a phone number, the topics a person cares about (their heart) and any special skills – maybe someone knows how to build a website.

Then *use* this information: invite people personally to events on their topic, or call them and ask for something specific. A spreadsheet is fine to start with; dedicated organising apps are also emerging.

### Tool 5: Sharing responsibility

<PullQuote>
  Only those who carry responsibility for a task are part of the movement.
</PullQuote>

Stable groups need responsibility to be shared – not carried by a handful of people.

- **Ask one-on-one:** Ask a room of 20 people who will take on a task and nobody raises their hand. Ask someone directly before or after the meeting and most people say yes.
- **Tandems:** Pair a new person with an experienced one – even for small tasks.
- **Delegate outwards:** There will always be a core group, and that's fine. But keep handing tasks to less engaged people, such as those in your chat group who rarely come to meetings: distributing flyers, collecting signatures, painting posters.

## Lessons from the discussion

- **Be approachable:** present yourselves as an initiative with a cause, not as a consortium of experts.
- **Find shared benefits:** a separate bike lane can also make things better for buses and cars.
- **Know your facts:** when streets are closed, <DefinitionTerm term="traffic evaporation" definition="When road space for cars is reduced, overall traffic often decreases instead of simply moving elsewhere." /> often means traffic decreases instead of shifting elsewhere.
- **Think creatively:** positioning an action as an art project can open new doors.
- **Don't be afraid of conversations.**

## Your own narrative and strategy

Every plan to win rests on a clear story. A narrative of engagement answers four questions:

- What are we working towards?
- Why is now a good window of opportunity?
- Who can build power with us?
- What change should our pressure bring about?

Wir machen Wien uses the Platz für Wien campaign as an example: many signatures, strong media coverage and Vienna's 2020 election were meant to create political pressure for a different transport policy. A local initiative can start smaller, but needs the same clarity.

<ExpandableBox lang="en">
  **Theories of change** are assumptions about how social and political change happens. They help you choose tactics deliberately instead of trying everything at once. Three perspectives are particularly useful for local initiatives: **major leaps** (a problem is redefined, new actors emerge, media attention opens a window), **grassroots** (people build power through collective action) and **group formation** (shared values, identity and norms keep a group able to act).
</ExpandableBox>

A small group should consistently use two or three suitable levers rather than ten half-heartedly.

<TableWrap>

| If you mainly need ... | Then ... fits better | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Attention | Agenda setting | Media image, petition, visible action |
| To build local power | Grassroots organising | One-on-one conversations, spectrum of allies, sharing roles |
| To stabilise a group | Group formation | Clarify values, rituals, shared language |

</TableWrap>

## Campaign structure

A simple campaign structure is enough to start:

1. **Goal:** What should change, and by when?
2. **Decision point:** Who can approve or prevent it?
3. **Target groups:** Who do we need to activate, persuade or put under pressure? (Use the spectrum of allies!)
4. **Milestones:** Which visible intermediate goals demonstrate progress?
5. **Tasks:** Who handles research, outreach, actions, documentation, media and political discussions?
6. **Rhythm:** When does the group meet, when does it communicate publicly, and when does it evaluate?
