Chapter 6: Work plan and sources

This chapter brings together the tools from chapters 2–5 as a flexible work plan for a new or existing neighbourhood initiative.

A four-week start-up plan

You don’t need a finished master plan to get started. Use these four weeks to understand your neighbourhood, bring people together, and take a first step towards change. Adjust the pace to your group and local circumstances: building relationships and winning lasting improvements can take longer.

Week 1: Get to know your neighbourhood

Choose your area. Mark a street, junction, or neighbourhood on a map. If you’re considering a superblock, look at the wider area and how through traffic moves across it.

Listen and observe. Walk through the area and talk to people who live, work, or spend time there. What feels unsafe, uncomfortable, or missing? What do people value? Document a starting point. Take photos, mark problem spots on a map, and choose one or two things to observe or count, such as traffic, pedestrian movements, or how many people stop.

Bring a core team together. Find a few neighbours who want to help. Share initial roles, set up a communication channel, and agree on your next meeting.

Try: Use Street Shit Bingo or the Leiwande Straßen Radar on your walk. For more ideas, see the power of superblocks and monitoring.

By the end of the week: You have a defined area, an initial picture of the problems, and a small group ready to continue.

Week 2: Build a shared vision and strategy

Create alternatives together. Use a street plan, the Street Design Table, or a simple sketch to explore how the space could be used differently. Think about small changes as well as a longer-term vision.

Set a clear goal. What should change, where, and by when? Turn your vision into a concrete first demand. Map your allies. Use the spectrum of allies to identify people and groups already on your side, those who might support you, and those you have not yet reached. Choose one group you could move one step closer to you.

Find the decision point. Who can approve, implement, or influence your demand? Note any upcoming meetings or opportunities to raise it.

Plan to win. Decide which two or three activities best fit your goal: for example, personal conversations to build local support, a visible action to attract attention, or a meeting with the district.

Try: Use the street design process and the spectrum of allies and campaign structure.

By the end of the week: You have a shared vision, a first demand, and a simple strategy for building support.

Week 3: Invite people in and make the vision visible

Have one-on-one conversations. Ask people what matters to them, listen to their experiences, and make a concrete invitation: join a meeting, help with an action, or share an idea.

Plan a small public activity. A street walk, design workshop, count, or playful intervention can make the issue visible and give people a way to participate.

Connect the action to your demand. Show both the problem and a possible alternative. For example, use a Street Banner or temporary seating to invite people to imagine a different street. Check what is needed. Clarify permissions, access for disabled people, materials, and responsibilities before acting. Ask allies to take on specific tasks, and pair new volunteers with more experienced people where useful.

Collect feedback. Invite participants and passers-by to share their views. Make clear what can still change and how you will use their input.

Try: Combine tactical urbanism with the head, heart, hand approach: connect what moves people to a clear plan and a concrete way to help.

By the end of the week: More people know about your initiative and have had a chance to shape or support it.

Week 4: Share what you learned and plan the next step

Review your evidence. Bring together observations, counts, photos, and feedback. If you tested an intervention, compare it with your starting point using the same method where possible.

Tell the story. Summarise the problem, your proposed solution, and what people experienced. A short page with a map, a few figures, photos, and quotes can be enough.

Follow up with participants. Share what you heard, what you will change, and what happens next. Invite people to take on a role that fits their interests.

Take your demand to decision-makers. Choose a suitable next move: a meeting, an open letter, a petition, a district motion, or a media story. Be clear about who needs to act and what you are asking them to do.

Set the next milestone. Agree on a date, assign responsibilities, and decide how you will track progress, including political responses and commitments.

Try: Use the minimal monitoring plan to connect evidence with your story and learn what to adjust.

By the end of the week: You have a clearer demand, evidence to support it, and a next step that people can work towards together.

Keep going

Change rarely follows a straight line. Keep listening, testing and building relationships. Return to your plan as you learn: What is working? Who else needs to be involved? What is the next useful step?

Guiding principle

Good initiatives combine four things: a clear demand, a visible local group, a viable spatial solution, and evidence that change is needed and achievable.

Final check before the next step

Phrase your next message so it contains all four elements: demand, group, solution, and evidence. If one is missing, outsiders will find the story harder to understand.
