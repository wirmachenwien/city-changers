Chapter 5: Monitoring: making impact visible

Monitoring means documenting progress and impact so that an initiative can learn, communicate and make political arguments. It does not need to be complicated – and it doesn't have to be left to experts. Self-initiated research has its own validity: it shows decision-makers where to look and lets them see a street through a different lens.

Connect data and storytelling.

says Zala from Prostorož.

Four ways to collect data

Prostorož in Ljubljana combines different methods depending on the question – and on the people it wants to reach.

1. Surveying

Ask people directly how they use a place. When Prostorož surveyed visitors to University Medical Centre Ljubljana about how they got there, the answer was clear: 64% came by car – a strong starting point for a discussion about access and mobility.

2. Mapping

Let people mark places on a map: where it is too hot, too dangerous, too loud – or where they like to spend time. Free apps such as Canvis or Mapperoni make hot spot mapping easy. Timing matters: if you are mapping urban heat, launch the survey on days above 30 °C, when people feel the problem most.

3. Collective mind maps

Collecting ideas together on a large sheet is fun and brings out a wide range of views. Keep in mind that the results are harder to translate into formal data.

4. Combining physical and digital

Put something into the street and ask for feedback at the same time. In Ljubljana, test benches were set up and passers-by could submit feedback on them – the intervention itself became the survey.

Observing life on the street

Counting and observing show what actually happens in a street – not just what people say.

Pedestrian flows

On a busy street in Ljubljana, volunteers counted pedestrians at 15-minute intervals and noted the directions they walked in. They also mapped where people stayed – and found that people could only stand, because there was nowhere to sit.

Park Beat

Which car parks around the city centre are actually used? Observations showed that at peak times, some had more cars than marked spaces, while others had spare capacity. Comparing this with the economic activity of nearby shops helps challenge the assumption that every parking space is essential for business.

Public Life app

The free Urban95 Public Life app, developed by Gehl and the Van Leer Foundation, is based on the Public Life Data Protocol. Read more about the Urban95 Public Life Data Framework.

Plan for 5–6 volunteers per street and an observation window from 5am to midnight. You don't need many days, but you will need a spreadsheet to analyse the results.

What can be measured

Simple indicators work well for superblocks, tactical urbanism and street design:

Traffic volumes: How many cars, bicycles and pedestrians use a section?

Speed and conflicts: Where do people drive too fast, and where do dangerous situations arise?

Places to spend time: Do people stop or sit down, do children play, do conversations emerge?

Parking: Which spaces are actually used, and when?

Climate and comfort: Is there shade, greenery, or drinking water? Are people exposed to urban heat or noise?

Support: How many people sign, attend meetings, share content or offer help?

Political progress: What motions, discussions, commitments or administrative reviews are taking place?

From data to change

Data alone rarely convinces anyone. Prostorož’s approach combines three steps: activating, envisioning, and advocating. Findings become convincing when people can see and experience them. Visualisations that respond directly to what the research revealed make the case far better than a table of numbers, and a temporary action can turn results into something tangible: in one action, kayak stands, a red fabric roof, and a few folding chairs turned five parking spaces into a place to spend time.

Four lessons from Ljubljana

First. Connect data and storytelling. Numbers become powerful when they are part of a story people recognise.

Second. Combine physical interventions with long-term visions. A temporary action shows what is possible; the vision explains where it leads.

Third. Learn from the other side. Borrow from commercial marketing: strong images, simple messages, and good timing.

Fourth. Take a lot of photos. You will need them – for reports, social media, press and political talks.

A minimal monitoring plan

First. Document the baseline: photos, a map, a short problem note, and an initial count.

Second. Define a target: for example, less through traffic, more safe crossings, or more supporters.

Third. Choose a method: survey, mapping, traffic or pedestrian count, observation, street radar or before-and-after photos.

Fourth. Pick the right moment: heat on hot days, traffic at rush hour, play after school.

Fifth. Assign responsibilities: one person coordinates the data, one takes photos, and one collects quotes and contacts.

Sixth. Present results: One page is often enough – the problem, method, key figures, photos, and your demand.

Seventh. Learn: What needs adjusting in your demand, action or alliance-building?

Start small

Robust monitoring does not need to be scientifically perfect. To start, a repeatable method is often enough: same place, same time, same observation question.

Monitoring as mobilisation

Monitoring can itself be an action. A traffic count on the street gets people talking. Test benches with a feedback form invite passers-by to have their say. A map of hot or dangerous spots can attract new supporters – and every person who joins a count is a potential new active ally.
