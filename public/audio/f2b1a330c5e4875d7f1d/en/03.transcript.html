3. Tactical urbanism and street design

Chapter 3: Tactical urbanism and street design

Tactical urbanism means low-cost, surface-level interventions in urban space. They are usually temporary, but can become permanent, making alternatives visible and accelerating political action towards a permanent redesign. They can start conversations, help communities test ideas, and build support for longer-term change.

Basic principles

Tactical urbanism works particularly well when five conditions come together:

The measure is rooted locally and solves a specific problem.

It can be implemented quickly and carries little risk.

It demonstrates a longer-term change on a small scale.

It builds relationships between the neighbourhood, initiatives, public administration and politicians.

It generates useful experience: what works, what needs adjusting, and who uses the space?

Check before every action

Can a passer-by recognise within a minute which problem the action highlights and which better use it proposes?

Tools

Tactical urbanism tools change the street at the surface level. They include:

Barriers such as bollards, concrete elements, or planter rings

Greenery and planting elements

Street furniture

Surface design using colour

Signs

Events

Quality lies in the appropriate use of an object, rather than the object itself: a painted surface is only effective if it changes behaviour, helps people find their way, or makes a new use plausible.

Tactical urbanism in practice: examples from Graz and Vienna

A practical process

1. Start with people’s experiences

Ask what makes a street feel welcoming, safe, or useful. Personal memories – such as the streets people played in as children – can open up a conversation about what public space could offer today.

2. Identify who uses the street and what they need

Consider children, older people, wheelchair users, cyclists, pedestrians, local businesses, delivery services, and people who live nearby. Trees and other elements of the urban environment also have specific needs, such as enough space to grow.

3. Observe and assess the street together

A simple street audit can help people discuss questions such as: Is there somewhere to sit? Is it noisy? How easy is it to cross? How is space divided between different forms of transport? The Leiwande Straßen Radar, presented by Wir machen Wien, is one example of a tool for assessing a street across multiple categories.

4. Make a shared vision tangible

A scale model or design table lets participants rearrange a street and explore different possibilities together. Temporary paint, movable seating, planters, or full-size banners can show how a different street layout might feel in real life.

5. Test, listen, and follow up

Use a temporary intervention to gather reactions from residents and passers-by. Share what you learned, explain how feedback will influence the next steps, and connect the experiment to discussions with the local authority.

What can a temporary intervention achieve?

Prostorož’s examples from Ljubljana show how simple materials and temporary changes can create new places to sit, meet, or spend time outdoors. In one project, a street was reimagined as a small public place using seating, colour, and plants. In another, a temporary redesign near schools created a safer route and a place for children to spend time after school while a permanent solution was still being decided.

These examples also show that temporary can still be long-lasting. Choose materials to match the purpose: lightweight elements can work well for a brief test, while a pilot that may remain in place for years needs robust, repairable materials.

What to keep in mind

The process matters as much as the physical intervention. If people are only asked for feedback and then hear nothing about how their feedback was used, participation can feel tokenistic. Be transparent about what can change, involve affected people early, and keep them informed as plans develop. Tactical urbanism works best when an experiment leads to learning – and when that learning has a clear path towards lasting, inclusive improvements.
