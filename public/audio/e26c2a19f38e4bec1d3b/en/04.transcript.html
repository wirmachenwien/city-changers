Chapter 4: Organising and strategies for change

Organising is more than reach. It is not just about how many people see something, but why they are willing to act together – and what each of them can actually do. Big changes have rarely come from the top down: women's right to vote, for example, was won by people who organised themselves around a shared cause. The same goes for liveable streets.

If we want things to change, we always have to fight for them from the bottom up.

says Valentina from Changing Cities.

From one initiative to a movement

Changing Cities shows what this can look like. Since 2019, the Berlin organisation has supported residents in founding neighbourhood initiatives campaigning for Kiezblocks. Through workshops, events, and online formats, it shared its knowledge openly. Around 70 initiatives were founded, over 30 Kiezblocks have been politically approved, and 13 are already being implemented on the streets.

Berlin isn't a Kiezblock paradise yet – but every one of these streets shows what organised neighbours can achieve.

Five tools for organising

Tool 1: Head, heart, hand

Less a tool than a principle to keep in mind whenever you organise people:

Head: How can we win? Strategy, tactics, arguments.

Heart: What moves people? A grandchild's unsafe route to school, a childhood memory of playing in the street, the fear of getting sick from air pollution.

Hand: What is our lever to win, and how can people take action? Always make a concrete offer – a task, an event, something to join.

A movement from below can only grow when you address all three.

Take a minute

What is your heart? What moves you to keep fighting for more liveable cities? Come back to it when you don’t know how to carry on.

Tool 2: Spectrum of allies

Who are you actually trying to reach? Initiatives often focus too much on their opponents. George Lakey's spectrum of allies (Training for Change) sorts the people around you into five groups:

A semicircle divided into five groups, with arrows pointing from active opposition towards active allies.

The aim is not to turn everyone into active allies at once. The key is to move one group one step closer to you. Start on the allies' side and leave the opposition aside for now.

Group: Active allies. What to do: Build strong relationships – above all through personal encounters such as meetings, events, and workshops. Give them something concrete to do.. Group: Passive allies. What to do: Find out what keeps them passive, then offer a small, concrete step – like baking a cake for the next play street (a street temporarily closed so children can play).. Group: Neutral. What to do: They can shift either way. Introduce yourselves: door-knocking, an info stand in your street, flyers..

The opposition is often loud, which makes it seem big. But which group is usually the largest? The neutrals – so focus on them and on your passive allies.

Try it with your initiative

Print the spectrum or draw it on a large sheet. Write the actors around your initiative on sticky notes – local politicians, schools, cafés, other initiatives – and place them on the spectrum. Then decide on your first step.

Example: In the founding workshop of a German superblock initiative, the group placed a local school – parents and children – between "passive ally" and "neutral". They organised an action day in front of the school. People got to know the initiative, and some joined afterwards.

Reflection question. What is the aim of the spectrum of allies? Convincing the active opposition first. Moving one group one step closer to you. Turning everyone into active allies at once. Pause to think. Answer: Moving one group one step closer to you.

Tool 3: One-on-one conversations

One face-to-face conversation is worth more than five WhatsApp messages.

Conversations stick in people's minds. They help you find out what moves someone, what makes them angry or hopeful – and win them over to your plan to win.

When? At the start of an initiative, to find out which issues really matter in the neighbourhood. Later, invite people to an assembly or ask them to collect signatures, to turn passive allies into active allies.

Where? At the door – or as "street talks" at the bus stop, in front of the supermarket, on the playground.

How? Follow these six steps – with example sentences (select to expand):

1. Introduction

"Hi, my name is … I live nearby, and I'm part of the neighbourhood initiative. Today we're talking to lots of people in our neighbourhood."

2. Find out what moves the person

Ask how they feel about the situation – "How do you feel about the traffic in our neighbourhood? What would you like to change?" Then go deeper: "What does that mean for you, your family, and your daily life?"

3. Create urgency

Help them see that the status quo is not a given: "Who do you think is responsible? What’s keeping the problem from being fixed? Don't you think it's unfair that cars get so much space and pedestrians so little?"

4. Your plan to win

Only now is it your turn to talk: "You said … Many people we talked to feel the same. That is why we decided to take action." Explain what your initiative is planning – a demonstration, a petition, talks with local politicians – and make clear: this will only work if we do it together.

5. The ask

A direct question: "Will you join? Can I count on you?"

6. Next step

Always end with a concrete agreement: "See you at the meeting on …?" or "I'll add you to the group now."

Golden rule: 20% talking, 80% listening. Go in pairs, prepare a short guideline and practise with a partner beforehand. Always take notes – and collect contact details.

Tool 4: Contact management

Contact management sounds basic, but it is often underestimated. Collect more than an email address: a phone number, the topics a person cares about (their heart), and any special skills – maybe someone knows how to build a website.

Then use this information: invite people personally to events about the topics they care about, or call them and ask for something specific. A spreadsheet is fine to start with; dedicated organising apps are also emerging.

Tool 5: Sharing responsibility

Only those who carry responsibility for a task are part of the movement.

Stable groups need responsibility to be shared – not carried by a handful of people.

Ask one-on-one: Ask a room of 20 people who will take on a task: nobody raises a hand. Ask someone directly before or after the meeting, and most people say yes.

Tandems: Pair a new person with an experienced one – even for small tasks.

Delegate outwards: There will always be a core group, and that's fine. But keep handing tasks to less engaged people, such as those in your chat group who rarely come to meetings: distributing flyers, collecting signatures, painting posters.

Lessons from the discussion

Be approachable: present yourselves as an initiative with a cause, not as a consortium of experts.

Find shared benefits: a separate bike lane can also make things better for buses and cars.

Know your facts: when streets are closed, traffic evaporation often occurs: overall traffic decreases instead of shifting elsewhere.

Think creatively: positioning an action as an art project can open new doors.

Don't be afraid of talking to strangers.

Your own narrative and strategy

Every plan to win rests on a clear story. A narrative of engagement answers four questions:

What are we working towards?

Why is now a good window of opportunity?

Who can build power with us?

What change should our pressure bring about?

Wir machen Wien uses the Platz für Wien campaign as an example: many signatures, strong media coverage, and the 2020 Vienna election were meant to create political pressure for a different transport policy. A local initiative can start smaller, but needs the same clarity.

Theories of change are assumptions about how social and political change happens. They help you choose tactics deliberately instead of trying everything at once. Three perspectives are particularly useful for local initiatives: major leaps (significant progress becomes possible when a problem is redefined, new actors emerge, or a window of opportunity opens), grassroots (people build power through collective action), and group formation (shared values, identity, and norms keep a group able to act).

A small group should consistently use two or three suitable levers rather than pursuing ten half-heartedly.

The table below lists practical approaches for different needs. Agenda setting means making an issue publicly visible; it is not the same as making major leaps in progress.

If you mainly need…: Attention. … this fits better: Agenda setting. Example: Striking image for the media, petition, visible action. If you mainly need…: To build local power. … this fits better: Grassroots organising. Example: One-on-one conversations, spectrum of allies, sharing roles. If you mainly need…: To stabilise a group. … this fits better: Group formation. Example: Clarify values, rituals, shared language.

Campaign structure

A simple campaign structure is enough to start:

First. Goal: What should change, and by when?

Second. Decision point: Who can approve or prevent it?

Third. Target groups: Who do we need to activate, persuade, or put under pressure? (Use the spectrum of allies!)

Fourth. Milestones: Which visible intermediate goals demonstrate progress?

Fifth. Tasks: Who handles research, outreach, actions, documentation, media, and political discussions?

Sixth. Rhythm: When does the group meet, when does it communicate publicly, and when does it evaluate?
